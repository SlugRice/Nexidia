Overall verdict

This set has major plausibility problems. The policy manual contains a clear New York registration-cycle error, while the SOP has impossible document chronology and questionable OpenMRS duplicate-handling logic.

Highest-confidence concerns

Physician registration cycle is wrong
 A03_Credentialing_Policy_Manual_Excerpt.docx states that New York physicians register every two years while other professions register every three years. New York generally uses triennial professional-registration periods, and NYSED describes the expected period as three years or two years depending on the profession. The available secondary source specifically identifies physicians as triennial. A New York credentialing specialist would likely catch this immediately.

The SOP predates the facts and authorities it relies upon
 A06_Credentialing_Reconciliation_SOP.docx is effective November 18, 2024, but describes what the March 3, 2025 cutover ETL “did,” specifies the resulting records as field-for-field identical, cites a March 2025 migration memo, cites a May 2025 crosswalk, and references a June 2025 committee charter revision. It is written as though all those later events and documents already existed. This is the strongest synthetic-document tell in the set.

The policy has the same chronology problem
 A03_Credentialing_Policy_Manual_Excerpt.docx carries a September 2024 revision but applies to Winston after a March 3, 2025 acquisition and is subordinate to a Credentials Committee Charter revised in June 2025. A retrospectively updated excerpt could contain later material, but then its revision date and revision history would need to reflect that update.

“Informational Person Attribute” that blocks scheduling and claims is contradictory
 The Registration Lapse Administrative Hold is labeled informational while it suspends scheduling and claim release. That is an active operational control. It could be represented by an attribute, but an experienced reviewer would expect enforcement interfaces, authorized users, effective dating, override handling, downstream acknowledgment, and failure monitoring.

Potentially unsafe claim-hold logic
 The policy proposes holding claims for services rendered on or after the registration lapse date. That may be appropriate as an internal precaution, but it is too categorical for a credentialing policy standing alone. Billing disposition can depend on practitioner type, service, supervision, billing arrangement, payer policy, enrollment status, and applicable law. The policy does not indicate that Claims, Revenue Cycle, Legal, or payer-contract specialists determine final disposition.

A06_Credentialing_Reconciliation_SOP.docx

Beyond the chronology failure:

Wrong level of ownership: This is primarily a migration data-governance and application-administration procedure, but the Office of Credentialing owns the technical record-voiding process.
Person versus Provider remains confused: The SOP voids a Provider record, then places the retired Provider UUID into a Person Attribute. That can be customized, but the entity relationship and cardinality are not explained.
No actual merge analysis: It prohibits any consolidation function and simply voids one record. There is no validation of linked encounters, orders, schedules, user accounts, provider attributes, payer records, or credentialing evidence before deactivation.
Misleading traceability claim: Adding the old UUID to the surviving record does not itself redirect a downstream system still using the retired UUID. The downstream system must be programmed to recognize that cross-reference.
OpenMRS capability claim is too absolute: “No such consolidation function exists in OpenMRS” is a product-level claim presented without version, module, or local implementation scope.
Weak approval control: A Senior Ops Analyst can perform the void without prior technical approval even though a mistaken action could affect operational provider identity.
Indefinite retention is unusually absolute: The document says the voided record remains in OpenMRS indefinitely and cannot be archived through routine retention. A real policy would normally defer to an approved retention schedule rather than create perpetual retention inside an SOP.
The population is frozen before it exists: It reports 17 alias records “as of the 11/2024 revision date,” even though the described Winston migration and acquisition occur in March 2025.
A03_Credentialing_Policy_Manual_Excerpt.docx

Additional concerns:

Overbroad credentialing scope: The manual groups medical-staff practitioners, employed clinicians, allied health professionals, technologists, payer enrollment, scheduling, billing, and claims controls under one credentialing policy. A large health system would more commonly divide these across medical staff, HR, payer enrollment, revenue cycle, compliance, and IT governance.
NPI language is overly absolute: “Each practitioner holds exactly one” Type 1 NPI is a useful operational expectation, but duplicate-NPI conditions and enumeration errors can occur. The document itself later acknowledges a provider might genuinely hold two.
Type 1 NPI is overextended as a universal identity key: Not every employee, technologist, or person subject to internal qualification verification necessarily needs an individual NPI.
Payer enrollment has two competing governing sources: Section 6 says the governing source may be either an OpenMRS attribute or the payer’s enrollment system. That leaves no clear authoritative source when they disagree.
Privilege scope is structurally thin: Two fields, a free-text scope and an effective date, are insufficient to operationalize detailed privileges across facilities and procedures.
Registration lapse response is very formalized: Automatically preparing a “precautionary-suspension recommendation” for every lapse may be locally adopted, but the terminology feels invented rather than standard medical-staff language.
Public Health Law reference is overbroad: Section 2803-e addresses specified incidents involving professional misconduct, disciplinary actions, patient safety, and related privilege or employment actions. A registration lapse does not automatically establish that one of those reporting conditions exists. Legal review is sensible, but the policy makes the statute sound more routinely implicated than its listed triggers support.
Policy owner may draw scrutiny: A system-wide credentialing manual owned by Compliance rather than Medical Staff Services, Credentialing, or Medical Affairs is possible, but atypical given the document’s extensive privileging content.
Missing conventional policy controls: The excerpt does not show review frequency, approval authority, approval history, regulatory references, related procedures, definitions for urgent or temporary actions, downtime handling, exception authority, or sanctions for noncompliance.
Strongest double-take items
November 2024 SOP describing a March 2025 migration as completed fact.
2024 documents citing 2025 revisions and artifacts.
Two-year physician registration cycle.
A supposedly informational attribute stopping scheduling and claims.
Credentialing staff directly voiding provider identities in OpenMRS.

One policy trying to govern credentialing, privileges, employment, enrollment, scheduling, and billing simultaneously.


======
2nd model
=======


All four NPIs across these two documents pass the 80840 check digit again, and A03 §2.1 actually publishes the algorithm — "begins with the digit 1 and validates against the Luhn check-digit rule when prefixed with 80840". No credentialing policy manual explains check-digit math. That sentence exists to teach a reader, not to govern staff, and it's the clearest authored-for-evaluation tell in the set.

The dating is broken in both documents

A06 is stamped Rev. 11/2024, Effective 11/18/2024, approved 11/18/2024. It then:

describes the failed ETL load in past tense (§1.2) for a cutover that hadn't happened
cites A05 at Rev. 03/2025 and the Credentials Committee Charter at Rev. 06/2025
states at §2.3 that "as of the 11/2024 revision date" the population consists of NPIs carrying both a Pre-close and a Post-acquisition row — rows that could not exist until 03/2025
fixes the alias population at seventeen "as of the 11/2024 revision date" (§5.4), the same 17 A05 reports in 03/2025

A03 has the same problem: Rev. 09/2024, but §1.2(b) speaks of Winston as "retained after the 03/03/2025 equity acquisition" and cites GOV-2023-012 Rev. 06/2025. A controlled document citing a future revision of another controlled document is the one error document-control people are trained to catch.

Related: "Supersedes: None (initial issuance)" paired with "Revision: 11/2024" is contradictory — an initial issuance is Rev. 1.0 or Rev. A. Neither document has a revision history table or a next-scheduled-review date, both of which are mandatory in a controlled set.

The Winston NPI contradicts the rest of the corpus

A03 §2.2 states Winston Imaging "continues to hold" organizational NPI 1342281247 because it survives as a wholly-owned subsidiary "retaining its legal entity, TIN, and organizational NPI". A08 calls the same number retired, informational-only, and not driving billing routing; A09 treats records carrying it as remediation exceptions; A43 audits for records that "still carry" it.

If the subsidiary survives with its TIN and NPI intact, those records are correct. There is no exception to remediate. This is the deepest hole in anything you've shown me, because it inverts the premise of two other documents.

Domain errors in A03

Precautionary suspension is the wrong instrument. §5.2(b) routes a lapsed NY registration to the MEC as a precautionary-suspension recommendation. Real bylaws handle lapsed licensure/registration by automatic relinquishment — ministerial, no committee action, no hearing rights, because there's nothing to adjudicate. "Precautionary suspension" is a term of art implying summary restriction with fair-hearing rights attached, and a suspension over 30 days is NPDB-reportable. Using it here manufactures due-process obligations and a reportable event out of an administrative lapse. A Medical Staff Services professional would stop hard on this.

Related: no summary suspension path at all. §1.5 says no one — not the CMO, not the MEC — may restrict privileges by unilateral action. Every real bylaw grants emergency summary suspension authority for imminent danger to patients. Its absence is not just unrealistic, it's a patient-safety gap a surveyor would cite.

The § 2803-e cite is wrong. PHL 2803-e requires hospitals to report suspensions, restrictions, or terminations of privileges to NYSED (or DOH for physicians and PAs) within 30 days, but only where the action relates to impairment, incompetence, malpractice, misconduct, or patient safety. A03 invokes it as the external reporting hook for a retrospective claims review. The obligation for claims billed during a lapse is the 60-day overpayment rule and, for Medicaid, OMIG self-disclosure. Also worth noting: there are two § 2803-e's in the PBH, and the other one is about returning unused medication in nursing homes — a bare cite without the subdivision is ambiguous.

Radiologic technologists are not NYSED. §3.3(b) puts "licensed technologists" in the NYSED three-year registration bucket. Rad techs in New York are licensed and registered by the Department of Health, Bureau of Environmental Radiation Protection — a different agency, a different primary source, and therefore a different PSV workflow than §3.4 prescribes. This compounds the A09 problem: your ARRT-MR row would be verified at DOH, and your RDMS sonographer isn't state-licensed in New York at all, so the entire license/registration framework in §3 doesn't apply to her.

Payer enrollment in the EMR, again. §6.2(e) names the OpenMRS attribute as a governing source for payer enrollment. Same issue I flagged in A08 M-003; now it's ratified at policy level.

"Informational" still contradicts itself. §2.4 defines the Registration Lapse hold as "an informational Person Attribute" that suspends scheduling and suspends claims release, then §5.2(a) correctly calls it "a scheduling and billing hold." The second phrasing is right; the first should go.

What a credentialing policy manual can't omit

A03 has no NPDB query requirement (initial and every two years), no OIG LEIE / SAM / OMIG exclusion screening, no stated recredentialing interval, no temporary or locum privileges, no FPPE/OPPE, no fair hearing and appeal procedure, and no delegated credentialing provisions despite the corpus referencing delegate contacts. Exclusion screening in particular is the single highest-liability control in credentialing, and its absence from a manual that devotes two full sections to status-dimension doctrine is conspicuous.

That doctrine is itself a tell. §6's six dimensions, recency resolution, and "no cross-dimension override" read as a rule specification for a reasoning task rather than policy language. So does the §5.1 paragraph explaining that a workflow Status of "Current" doesn't override an expired Registration_Expiry_Date — that's an instruction to whoever is being tested, dropped into a policy voice.

A06-specific

Person vs. Provider is conflated. §4.2 voids the Provider record; §4.4 attaches a Person Attribute. In OpenMRS these are distinct entities with distinct attribute tables. More importantly, the SOP never says whether the duplicate Provider rows point at one Person or two — which is the actual hard question, and determines whether you have a provider duplicate or a patient-style person duplicate.

Nothing repoints the dependent data. Voiding a Provider row in OpenMRS leaves encounters, orders, and encounter_provider rows referencing the voided provider. A real remediation SOP would address that in its own step. Section 3.1's assertion that "no such consolidation function exists in OpenMRS" is roughly true for providers, but stating a vendor's feature gap in a controlled policy is not how these are written — that belongs in a systems memo.

Governance of the document itself. Hoyt authored it and Hoyt approved it, with CCO concurrence dated the same day as issuance and effectiveness. No review interval, no second signature that isn't a concurrence. And §7.2's mandate that voided rows be retained "indefinitely" and never archived off-system will collide with any records retention schedule.

Population counts don't belong in an SOP. §2.3 and §5.4 hard-code the affected population into a controlled procedure. Counts drift; that's why A05 deliberately declined to list NPIs. The SOP does the opposite of what the memo it cites does.

§3.3's "single work session" rule — reconciliation must complete in one sitting, no interim state — is the kind of absolute that no operational SOP survives contact with. Real procedures define an acceptable in-flight state and a maximum duration.
